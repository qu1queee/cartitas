# The Moon — middle (8–11)

<!-- lang: en | age: middle | topic: space | subtopic: moon -->

Q: How far away is the Moon?
A: About 238,900 miles (384,400 km). That sounds huge, but it is still close enough for astronauts to reach in a few days.

---

C: The Moon's gravity is about [one-sixth] as strong as Earth's.

---

Q: What causes ocean tides?
A: Mostly the Moon's gravity pulling on Earth's oceans. The Sun pulls too, but the Moon has the bigger effect.

---

Q: Does the Moon make its own light?
A: No. The Moon reflects sunlight, like a bright mirror in the sky. The part we see lit up is sunlight bouncing off it.

---

Q: Why do we always see the same side of the Moon from Earth?
A: The Moon takes the same time to spin once as it does to orbit Earth. So the same face stays turned toward us.

# The Moon — middle (8–11)

<!-- age: middle | lang: en | topic: space | subtopic: moon -->

Q: What are the phases of the Moon?
A: They are the different shapes the Moon seems to have in the sky, like a thin crescent or a full circle. They change as the Moon orbits Earth and sunlight hits different parts of it.

---

C: From new Moon to the next new Moon takes about [29] days. That cycle is sometimes called a lunar month.

---

Q: Could you breathe on the Moon without a spacesuit?
A: No. The Moon has almost no air. You would need a spacesuit with oxygen, like the astronauts who visited the Moon.

---

Q: What is a lunar eclipse?
A: It happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon. Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, so it can look dim or reddish for a while.

---

Q: How long did the Apollo astronauts take to reach the Moon?
A: About three days each way by rocket. The whole trip there and back took roughly a week or more, including time on the surface.
